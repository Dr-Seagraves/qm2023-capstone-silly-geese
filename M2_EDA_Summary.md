KEY FINDINGS: 
-Correlations: Lobbying and firm performance move together positively in the data, but the relationship is moderate rather than overwhelming. Economically, this is consistenet with a policy-access channel: firms that spend more on lobbying may secure regulatory clarity, contract opportunities, or lower policy uncertainty, which can support stronger financial outcomes. 

-Optimal lag: The relationship appears strongest at an intermediate lag rather than contemporaneously, suggesting lobbying effects are delayed. The economic mechanism is implementation timing: policy influence occurs first, then operating and financial effects materialize after rules are finalized, budgets are allocated, or market behavior adjusts. 

-Group sensitivity: The association is notably stronger in larger and more policy-exposed firms, while weaker in smaller or less exposed groups. This matches a fixed-cost/intensity mechanism: larger firms can maintain dedicated government-relations capacity and target high-stakes policy areas, producing larger marginal returns to lobbying effort. 

-Outliers: A small number of extreme spenders and very large firms have high leverage in level-based relationships, inflating raw slopes. After log scaling or outlier-robust checks, the positive direction generally remains, implying concentration at the top affects magnitude more than sign. 

-Control patterns: Adding controls (size, industry, year effects, baseline financials) attenuates the lobbying coefficient but does not usually eliminate it. This supports a selection-plus-impact interpretation: stronger firms are more likely to lobby, but lobbying may still have an incremental association with outcomes beyond those baseline advantages.
